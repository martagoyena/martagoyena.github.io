Pericardiocentesis XR Training

How should a learner handle virtual medical instruments in XR? I compare controllers, hand tracking and haptic gloves in an immersive simulation of pericardiocentesis, and I gather feedback from healthcare professionals.

Medical XR training Multimodal interaction Haptic feedback User evaluation
View code on GitHub Watch the demo

Why this project

Pericardiocentesis is a clinical procedure that removes fluid from the space around the heart. It is hands-on and high-stakes, so a safe place to practise it is valuable.

In an XR simulator, the way a learner holds and moves virtual instruments is part of the learning experience. Controllers, tracked hands and haptic gloves each change how natural and precise that feels, so I want to test which one works best for this task instead of assuming.

What I want to find out

Four questions guide the study, each with the reason I am asking it.

  1. How do controllers, hand tracking and haptic gloves compare for handling virtual instruments in this procedure?

    Why: each device changes how natural and precise the task feels, and the best choice for training should be tested.

  2. How do these methods affect usability, workload, presence and comfort?

    Why: a training tool only helps if learners can focus on the procedure instead of fighting the interface.

  3. What can movement and interaction data show about how people carry out the task?

    Why: questionnaires capture opinions, while tracking data shows what users actually do.

  4. How do healthcare professionals judge each method for training?

    Why: clinicians know the real procedure, so their feedback tells us what is realistic and useful.

Three ways to interact

Controllers

A tracked, handheld input method for picking up and moving the virtual instruments.

Hand tracking

Users interact with their own tracked hands, without holding any device.

Haptic gloves

SenseGlove Nova 2 gloves add haptic feedback. Their setup and hand calibration are part of the workflow.

What I do

I work on the interaction and the evaluation. That means integrating the alternative input devices, handling their setup and calibration, and studying how users interact with the virtual instruments during the procedure.

The evaluation combines questionnaires with data about users’ movements and interactions, plus open feedback from the professionals who try it.

What I measure

  • Usability, with SUS
  • Workload, with NASA-TLX
  • Simulator sickness, with SSQ or SSSQ as applicable
  • Presence, with IPQ
  • Movement and interaction data
  • Open-ended feedback
